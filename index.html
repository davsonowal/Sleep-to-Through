Sleep & the human story

How we learned
to sleep differently

A short journey from firelit caves to lit-up screens — and a look at where you can still feel the old rhythms today.

Scroll to travel through the night ↓

Every human who has ever lived has spent roughly a third of their life asleep. What changes, generation to generation, is not the need for sleep but its shape — when it happens, where it happens, and what surrounds it.

Firelight, seasons, candlewax, gaslight, the light bulb, the phone screen: each one nudged sleep a little further from the sun. This section traces that drift, era by era, then points to a few places on the map where an older rhythm still survives.

circa 40,000 years ago

The firelit night

Hunter-gatherer bands, open air and cave shelters

Sleep followed the sun almost exactly. Researchers who studied present-day hunter-gatherer groups without electric light found people settling in a few hours after sunset and rising before sunrise, with no fixed bedtime at all — the body simply wound down as the fire burned low.

Temperature, not the clock, may have called the first humans to bed — sleep tended to begin as the night cooled, not at a fixed hour.
antiquity to late medieval period

The two sleeps

Farming villages and early cities, before cheap light

For much of pre-industrial history, a night wasn't always one block of sleep. Households often woke for an hour or so in the middle of the night — to tend a fire, talk quietly, pray, or simply lie awake — before returning to a "second sleep" until dawn. Diaries and court records from this period refer to it as an ordinary, unremarkable part of the night.

Homes were shared, cold, and dim. Sleep was communal by necessity — a whole family, sometimes a whole household, in one room.

1800s onward

The lit night

Gaslight, then the electric bulb

Artificial light rewrote the evening. Once a room could be lit cheaply after dark, wake time and work time stopped being tied to daylight, and the "second sleep" habit faded from mention within a couple of generations. Sleep consolidated into one block, pushed later, and started shrinking.

today

The lit-up night

Screens, shift work, and always-on cities

Now light comes from a device held inches from the face, work schedules ignore sunrise entirely, and a city never really goes dark. Average reported sleep in many industrialized countries sits well under eight hours, and a large share of adults describe their sleep as poor or insufficient on a regular basis.

The body clock hasn't changed since the firelit night — the light around it has.

where the old rhythms remain

Travel toward slower nights

A handful of places and traditions still keep something closer to the older pattern — worth knowing if this section becomes part of a wider itinerary.

柚

Onsen towns

Japan — hot spring villages

Evenings built around a hot bath and an early futon on tatami; towns quiet down not long after dinner, closer to the pre-electric rhythm than most modern cities.

☀

Siesta country

Spain, Southern Italy, Greece

A midday pause echoes the biphasic pattern many pre-industrial societies followed, splitting rest around the heat of the day rather than compressing it into one modern block.

✦

Desert camps

Sahara & Wadi Rum

Without ambient city light, the sky darkens fully and sleep tends to arrive early and heavy — a rare, direct experience of night as it looked before electricity.

✵

Nordic midnight sun & polar night

Norway, Iceland, Finland

Extreme seasonal light shows just how much sleep timing bends to available daylight — the same flexibility ancient humans relied on, seen at its most dramatic.

The takeaway for the itinerary

Sleep hasn't changed. Light has. Every stop above offers a night with less of it — a small, genuine way to let visitors feel a rhythm their ancestors knew by default.
